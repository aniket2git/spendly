> *Where the marks actually are. Read this before you attempt a single question.*



## 1.1 The Four Exams This Bank Covers



### 1.1.1 Why one bank works for all of them


DSSSB, KVS, NVS, EMRS and most state TGT/PGT Computer Science papers draw from **almost the same syllabus**: computer fundamentals, digital logic, architecture, operating systems, programming, data structures, DBMS, networks, web technologies, software engineering, plus teaching methodology.

What differs is the **packaging** - number of questions, time, whether there is a Tier-2, and how much weight the subject section carries. The subject content overlaps heavily, which is why a single well-organised bank serves all of them.


### 1.1.2 Pattern comparison



| Exam | Reported pattern | Negative marking |
|---|---|---|
| DSSSB TGT | One-tier CBT, **200 MCQs, 200 marks, 120 minutes**. Section A = 100 marks (General Awareness, Reasoning, Arithmetic, English, Hindi). Section B = 100 marks (Subject Concerned including Teaching Methodology) | **0.25** per wrong answer |
| KVS TGT | Written test reported at **180 marks**; the "Subject Concerned" part carries the **largest share**, with additional parts for language proficiency, general awareness, reasoning and pedagogy. Final merit combines the written test with an interview / professional competency test | 0.25 per wrong answer |
| NVS TGT | MCQ-based, **1 mark per question with 0.25 negative marking**. Recent notifications have described both a single 150-question paper and a two-tier structure, so the number of questions has varied between cycles | 0.25 per wrong answer |
| EMRS TGT | Multi-part paper including General Knowledge and Current Affairs, Reasoning Ability, and a dedicated **Knowledge of ICT** part (computer fundamentals, operating system basics, MS Office, shortcuts, abbreviations, networks, cyber security, internet), plus the subject and pedagogy | 0.25 per wrong answer |
| State TGT/PGT | Varies by state. Usually 100-200 MCQs with a subject section of 50-70% weight | Usually 0.25, some states have none |



> **NOTE: Verify the pattern yourself - this matters**
>
> Exam patterns for these bodies **change between recruitment cycles**, and the figures above come from secondary coaching and news sources rather than a single official document I could read end to end. Two of them openly disagree on the NVS question count.
>
> Before you build a time strategy around any of these numbers, download the **official notification PDF** for the specific cycle you are sitting and confirm: total questions, total marks, duration, sectional split, negative marking, and whether sectional qualifying marks apply.

### 1.1.3 What the marking scheme means for strategy


With **+1 for correct and -0.25 for wrong**, the arithmetic of guessing is fixed:


| Situation | Expected value per question | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| You know the answer | +1.00 | Attempt |
| You eliminated 2 of 4 options (50% chance) | 0.5(+1) + 0.5(-0.25) = **+0.375** | ATTEMPT - clearly worth it |
| You eliminated 1 of 4 options (33% chance) | 0.33(+1) + 0.67(-0.25) = **+0.16** | Attempt, marginally worth it |
| Pure blind guess (25% chance) | 0.25(+1) + 0.75(-0.25) = **+0.0625** | Barely positive. Skip if a distractor looks deliberately attractive |
| You have no idea and cannot eliminate anything | Effectively 0 or negative | SKIP |



> **TIP: The single most profitable habit**
>
> Do not aim to answer everything. Aim to **eliminate two options** on every question you are unsure about. That converts a coin-flip into a clearly positive-expectation attempt, and it is a trainable skill - it comes from knowing the *neighbouring* concepts, which is exactly what the explanations in this bank drill.

### 1.2.1 How these numbers were produced


Two independent inputs:

1. **Observed count.** I counted every subject question in the three DSSSB TGT Computer Science papers supplied, classified it by topic, and averaged per paper. These are hard counts from real papers.
2. **Syllabus cross-check.** I compared that distribution against the published topic lists for KVS, NVS and EMRS Computer Science to catch topics that DSSSB happened not to ask in those three papers but which sit squarely on the common syllabus.

Where the two disagree, the "Expected range" column widens. Treat the range as a planning tool, not a prophecy.

> **NOTE: Read the last three rows carefully - they decide your strategy**
>
> **Row 13 (Emerging technology).** The three DSSSB papers you shared barely touched AI, cloud, IoT or cyber security. But newer papers across all these bodies are adding them, and EMRS already has an explicit ICT and cyber-security component. I have therefore included a full section on it (Chapter 9.4) even though its historical DSSSB count is near zero. This is the one place where I am deliberately preparing you for a paper that may not look like the last one.
>
> **Row 15 (Physics / Accounting / Economics).** This is a DSSSB peculiarity - its Discipline sections pull from the wider graduate stream. KVS and NVS Computer Science papers generally do **not** do this. If you are sitting only KVS or NVS, you may de-prioritise this row. If you are sitting DSSSB, you cannot - it was worth 6 to 10 marks per paper.
>
> **Row 16 (Teaching Methodology).** The highest marks-per-hour-of-study in the entire syllabus, across every one of these exams. Most candidates under-prepare it because it feels less serious than Computer Science. That is exactly why it is the cheapest place to gain 15 marks.



### 1.2.3 Where to spend your study hours


If you have 100 hours left, this is roughly how to allocate them:


| Hours | Spend on | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| 20 | Teaching Methodology | Highest return per hour; almost pure recall |
| 18 | Operating Systems + Linux | Biggest CS block, very predictable question types |
| 16 | Computer Networks + Security | Second biggest, formula-driven and learnable |
| 16 | Programming output-tracing | Cannot be crammed - needs daily practice |
| 10 | Data Structures and Algorithms complexity tables | Pure recall, high frequency |
| 8 | DBMS and SQL | Compact syllabus, reliable marks |
| 6 | Digital Logic and Number Systems | Short, mechanical, quick wins |
| 6 | Everything else (SE, graphics, MIS, emerging tech) | Breadth cover, one pass each |



> **TIP: The mistake almost everyone makes**
>
> Candidates spend 60% of their time on the topic they already enjoy - usually programming - and arrive at the exam having never once revised Teaching Methodology or MIS. Your weakest *high-weightage* topic is always worth more than your strongest one. Rank your topics by (weightage x weakness) and study the top of that list, not the top of your comfort list.



## 1.3 How to Use This Question Bank



### 1.3.1 The method


1. Pick one chapter. **Cover the answers.** Attempt every question, writing your choice on paper.
2. Score yourself with the real scheme: +1, -0.25, 0.
3. Read **every** explanation, including for questions you answered correctly. The explanations teach the *neighbouring* concepts, which is where the next paper's questions come from.
4. Keep a "leak list" - one line per question you got wrong, naming the concept, not the question.
5. After finishing all chapters, your leak list is your revision syllabus. Nothing else.
6. Re-attempt each chapter after two weeks. Anything wrong twice needs the underlying theory re-read, not just the answer memorised.


### 1.3.2 What the labels mean



| Label | Meaning |

| [EXPECTED] | I wrote this question on a syllabus topic that recurs across DSSSB, KVS, NVS, EMRS and state TGT papers. It is **not** copied from any paper - it is a practice question built to the same pattern. |

| [TRAP] | A question most candidates get wrong, usually because two options are both plausible. Read the explanation twice. |
