1.1.2 Why Section A is where selections are won

Think about what these two halves are.

Section B is your subject. You studied it for three years at degree level. Every other candidate in the hall also studied it for three years. The marks there cluster tightly - most serious candidates score somewhere between 60 and 80, and pushing from 70 to 75 costs enormous effort.

Section A is the opposite. It is broad, shallow and highly repetitive. And most candidates barely touch it, because it feels less serious than their subject. The spread here is enormous - I have seen candidates score 35 and candidates score 88 on the same paper.

That spread is the selection. A candidate who scores 70 in Section B and 85 in Section A beats a candidate who scores 80 in Section B and 55 in Section A - by 20 marks.

NOTE: The honest version of the "95+" promise

Reasoning, Maths, English and Hindi are 80 marks, and they are almost entirely within your control. They are rule-based and finite. If you work through Parts 4, 5, 6 and 7 of this book properly and practise, scoring 74-78 out of those 80 is a realistic, repeatable outcome. That is the real engine of a high score.

General Awareness is 20 marks and only partly within your control. Static GK - history, geography, polity, economy, science - is learnable and this book covers it thoroughly. Current affairs is not learnable from any book, including this one, because the events have not happened yet when the book is written. You must add a daily source. I explain exactly how in Chapter 3.1, and I will not pretend otherwise.

So the honest promise is this: this book can take you to about 90, and a disciplined current-affairs habit on top of it can take you past 95. Any book that claims to do the whole 95 by itself, current affairs included, is not being straight with you.

1.2 Time Strategy Inside the Exam Hall

1.2.1 The 36-second problem

200 questions in 120 minutes is 36 seconds each. But the questions are not equal. A synonym takes 8 seconds. A time-and-work sum takes 90. A reading comprehension passage takes 4 minutes for 5 questions.

So you do not budget per question. You budget per section.

TIP: The order to attempt in

Do not attempt the paper top to bottom. Attempt it in increasing order of time cost:

Hindi, then English, then General Awareness, then your Section B subject, then Reasoning, then Mathematics.

Two reasons. First, you bank the certain marks before fatigue and clock-panic set in. Second, if you run out of time - which most candidates do - the questions you sacrifice are the slow ones you were least likely to get right anyway, not the fast ones you were certain about.

1.2.2 The two-pass method

*How to move through a section*

PASS 1  (fast sweep)
  - Read each question once.
  - If the answer arrives within your time budget, mark it and move on.
  - If it does not, FLAG IT and move on immediately. Do not linger.
  - Target: finish pass 1 using about 70% of the section's time.

PASS 2  (return sweep)
  - Go back only to the flagged questions.
  - For each, try to ELIMINATE two options.
  - Succeeded? Answer it - the expected value is positive.
  - Failed? Leave it blank and move on.

NEVER let one question consume more than 2 minutes. Ever.

1.2.3 The mathematics of guessing - learn this table

With +1 and -0.25, guessing is not free but it is not forbidden either. The decision is arithmetic, not courage.

Your situationChance of being rightExpected marksDecision
You know it100%+1.00Answer
You eliminated 3 of 4 (certain of two, unsure which)50%0.5(1) + 0.5(-0.25) = +0.375Answer - clearly worth it
You eliminated 2 of 433%0.33(1) + 0.67(-0.25) = +0.16Answer - still positive
You eliminated nothing (blind guess)25%0.25(1) + 0.75(-0.25) = +0.0625Marginal. Skip if any distractor looks deliberately attractive
You have a hunch but cannot eliminate anythingbelow 25%NegativeSkip
The rule that follows: never leave blank a question where you can eliminate two options. Never attempt a question where you can eliminate none.
NOTE: Why blind guessing is worse than the maths suggests

The +0.0625 figure assumes each of the four options is equally likely - a random lottery. Real exam papers are not random. Examiners deliberately build attractive wrong options that match a common misconception. So on a question you genuinely do not understand, your real chance of being right is often *below* 25%, because you will be drawn to the trap rather than picking uniformly.

That is why the practical rule is: eliminate, or skip. And it is why the "why the wrong options are wrong" explanations throughout this book matter more than the answers themselves - they are training your elimination instinct.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] Who is called the Father of the Indian Constitution?

  • (a) Jawaharlal Nehru
  • (b) Dr. Rajendra Prasad
  • (c) Dr. B. R. Ambedkar <-- CORRECT
  • (d) Mahatma Gandhi
Why: Ambedkar chaired the Drafting Committee. Note the distinction that examiners exploit: Dr. Rajendra Prasad was the President of the Constituent Assembly, and later India's first President.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] Which Article of the Constitution deals with the Right to Education?

  • (a) Article 19
  • (b) Article 21A <-- CORRECT
  • (c) Article 32
  • (d) Article 45
Why: Article 21A, inserted by the 86th Amendment, 2002, makes free and compulsory education for ages 6-14 a fundamental right. Article 45 is the related *Directive Principle* on early childhood care - a deliberate distractor.

Q. [EXPECTED] [TRAP] Which Fundamental Right was removed from the Constitution?

  • (a) Right to Equality
  • (b) Right to Property <-- CORRECT
  • (c) Right to Freedom
  • (d) Right against Exploitation
Why: The Right to Property was deleted as a Fundamental Right by the 44th Amendment (1978) and shifted to Article 300A as an ordinary legal right. So India now has six Fundamental Rights, not seven.

Q. [EXPECTED] The Rajya Sabha is a permanent house because:

  • (a) Its members serve for life
  • (b) It is never dissolved; one-third of members retire every two years <-- CORRECT
  • (c) It has no elections
  • (d) The President chairs it
Why: Its continuity is the key contrast with the Lok Sabha, which can be dissolved. Members serve 6-year terms staggered so a third retire biennially.

3.1 The Honest Chapter - Read This First

3.1.1 Why no book can give you current affairs

You asked me not to skip anything and to give this my best. The most useful thing I can do here is be straight with you rather than pad this Part with a list that looks impressive and expires.

Here is the problem in one sentence: a book is written at one moment, and your exam happens at another.

Every other Part of this book will still be correct in five years. Pythagoras will not change. Article 21A will not change. This Part starts decaying the day it is written.

Three consequences you need to accept:

  1. I cannot predict specific current-affairs questions. Anyone who claims to is guessing and dressing it up. What I *can* do is show you the categories that recur every single year, and those categories are genuinely stable.
  1. Some facts in this Part will already be out of date when you read it. Office holders change. Tournaments get won by someone else. Where I give a specific name or figure, I have flagged how to verify it.
  1. A daily habit will beat this chapter. Fifteen minutes a day for six months is worth more than this entire Part, because it covers the actual window your paper draws from.
NOTE: What I did and did not verify

I searched for current affairs material while writing this and found the standard aggregator sources, monthly capsules and summit round-ups. But I want to be precise about the limits of that:

I have not independently verified individual current-affairs facts against primary government sources, and current-affairs aggregators contain errors. So rather than transcribing a list of names and dates that I cannot stand behind and that you would be memorising on trust, I have built this Part as a framework plus a verification method.

Where I do name something specific, I say where to check it. Where I am not confident, I say so instead of filling the gap. A wrong fact learned confidently is worse than a gap you know you have - in an exam with negative marking, false confidence actively costs you marks.

4.1 The Master Formula Sheet

4.1.1 Read this chapter first, and revise it weekly

Everything in Part 4 rests on these formulas. If you learn nothing else from this Part, learn this chapter. Most candidates lose maths marks not because a question was hard but because they could not recall the formula in the 60 seconds available.

*[FORMULA] PERCENTAGE*

   x% of y            = (x/100) x y
   x as a % of y      = (x/y) x 100
   Percentage change  = (change / ORIGINAL value) x 100

   If a value increases by x% then decreases by x%,
      the NET result is always a DECREASE of (x^2/100)%.

   Successive changes of a% and b%:
      net % change = a + b + (ab/100)     (use signs: decrease is negative)

   If A is x% more than B, then B is [100x/(100+x)]% less than A.
   If A is x% less than B, then B is [100x/(100-x)]% more than A.

*[FORMULA] PROFIT AND LOSS*

   Profit         = SP - CP
   Loss           = CP - SP
   Profit %       = (Profit / CP) x 100        <- always on COST PRICE
   Loss %         = (Loss / CP) x 100

   SP = CP x (100 + Profit%) / 100
   CP = SP x 100 / (100 + Profit%)

   Discount       = MP - SP
   Discount %     = (Discount / MP) x 100      <- always on MARKED PRICE

   Successive discounts of a% and b%:
      single equivalent discount = a + b - (ab/100)

   If an article is sold at two different prices with the SAME
   percentage profit and loss, there is always a NET LOSS of (x^2/100)%.

*[FORMULA] SIMPLE AND COMPOUND INTEREST*

   SI  = (P x R x T) / 100
   Amount = P + SI

   CI: Amount A = P (1 + R/100)^T
   CI = A - P

   Compounded half-yearly: rate becomes R/2, time becomes 2T
   Compounded quarterly  : rate becomes R/4, time becomes 4T

   For 2 years:  CI - SI = P (R/100)^2
   For 3 years:  CI - SI = P (R/100)^2 x (300 + R) / 100

   Money DOUBLES in 100/R years at simple interest.

*[FORMULA] RATIO AND PROPORTION*

   a : b = c : d   means   ad = bc   (product of extremes = product of means)

   If a:b = c:d, then:
      componendo   (a+b):b = (c+d):d
      dividendo    (a-b):b = (c-d):d

   To divide amount X in ratio a:b:c ->
      shares are  Xa/(a+b+c),  Xb/(a+b+c),  Xc/(a+b+c)

   Mean proportional between a and b = sqrt(ab)
   Third proportional to a and b     = b^2 / a
   Fourth proportional to a, b, c    = bc / a

*[FORMULA] AVERAGE*

   Average = (sum of all observations) / (number of observations)

   Sum of first n natural numbers        = n(n+1)/2
   Average of first n natural numbers    = (n+1)/2
   Sum of squares of first n numbers     = n(n+1)(2n+1)/6
   Sum of cubes of first n numbers       = [n(n+1)/2]^2
   Average of first n odd numbers  = n
   Average of first n even numbers = n + 1

   If the average of n numbers is A and one number x is replaced by y,
      the new average = A + (y - x)/n

*[FORMULA] TIME AND WORK*

   If A does a job in 'a' days, A's one-day work = 1/a

   A and B together take   ab/(a+b) days
   A, B, C together take   1 / (1/a + 1/b + 1/c) days

   If A is twice as fast as B, then A:B time ratio = 1:2
      and A:B work ratio = 2:1

   PIPES AND CISTERNS: an inlet is POSITIVE work, an outlet is NEGATIVE.
      Filled together in  1 / (1/a - 1/b)  when b is the leak.

   M1 D1 H1 / W1  =  M2 D2 H2 / W2
      (men x days x hours per day, divided by work done)

*[FORMULA] TIME, SPEED AND DISTANCE*

   Speed = Distance / Time

   UNIT CONVERSION (memorise these two):
      km/hr to m/s : multiply by 5/18
      m/s to km/hr : multiply by 18/5

   Average speed for equal DISTANCES at speeds x and y = 2xy/(x+y)
      (this is the harmonic mean - NOT the simple average)
   Average speed for equal TIMES at speeds x and y = (x+y)/2

   TRAINS
      Train passing a POLE   : distance = length of train
      Train passing a PLATFORM: distance = train length + platform length
      Same direction (overtaking): relative speed = difference of speeds
      Opposite direction (crossing): relative speed = sum of speeds

   BOATS AND STREAMS
      Downstream speed = boat speed + stream speed
      Upstream speed   = boat speed - stream speed
      Boat speed   = (down + up) / 2
      Stream speed = (down - up) / 2

*[FORMULA] MENSURATION - 2D*

   Square      : area = a^2            perimeter = 4a       diagonal = a x sqrt2
   Rectangle   : area = l x b          perimeter = 2(l+b)   diagonal = sqrt(l^2+b^2)
   Triangle    : area = (1/2) x base x height
   Equilateral : area = (sqrt3/4) a^2  height = (sqrt3/2) a
   Circle      : area = pi r^2         circumference = 2 pi r
   Parallelogram: area = base x height
   Rhombus     : area = (1/2) d1 x d2
   Trapezium   : area = (1/2)(sum of parallel sides) x height
   Sector      : area = (theta/360) pi r^2

*[FORMULA] MENSURATION - 3D*

   Cube        : volume = a^3       total surface = 6a^2    diagonal = a x sqrt3
   Cuboid      : volume = lbh       total surface = 2(lb+bh+hl)
                 diagonal = sqrt(l^2+b^2+h^2)
   Cylinder    : volume = pi r^2 h  curved surface = 2 pi r h
                 total surface = 2 pi r (r + h)
   Cone        : volume = (1/3) pi r^2 h    slant l = sqrt(r^2 + h^2)
                 curved surface = pi r l    total = pi r (r + l)
   Sphere      : volume = (4/3) pi r^3      surface = 4 pi r^2
   Hemisphere  : volume = (2/3) pi r^3      total surface = 3 pi r^2

*[FORMULA] NUMBER SYSTEM*

   Dividend = Divisor x Quotient + Remainder

   HCF x LCM = product of the two numbers
   HCF of fractions = HCF of numerators / LCM of denominators
   LCM of fractions = LCM of numerators / HCF of denominators

   DIVISIBILITY RULES
      2  : last digit is even
      3  : sum of digits divisible by 3
      4  : last TWO digits divisible by 4
      5  : last digit is 0 or 5
      6  : divisible by both 2 and 3
      8  : last THREE digits divisible by 8
      9  : sum of digits divisible by 9
      11 : (sum of odd-position digits) - (sum of even-position digits)
           is 0 or divisible by 11

4.2 Number System, LCM and HCF

4.2.1 Questions

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] The LCM of two numbers is 495 and their HCF is 5. If their sum is 100, find their difference.

  • (a) 10
  • (b) 10 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 46
  • (d) 54
Why: Use HCF x LCM = product: 5 x 495 = 2475. So the two numbers have sum 100 and product 2475. Solving x(100-x) = 2475 gives x^2 - 100x + 2475 = 0, which factorises as (x-45)(x-55) = 0. The numbers are 45 and 55, so the difference is 10.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] Find the largest number that divides 62, 132 and 237 leaving the same remainder in each case.

  • (a) 30
  • (b) 35 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 25
  • (d) 40
Why: Method: when the remainder is the same but unknown, take the differences and find their HCF. Differences: 132-62 = 70, 237-132 = 105, 237-62 = 175. HCF of 70, 105 and 175 is 35. (Check: 62 = 35x1 + 27, 132 = 35x3 + 27, 237 = 35x6 + 27. Same remainder 27.)

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] What is the least number which when divided by 5, 6, 7 and 8 leaves remainder 3 in each case?

  • (a) 843
  • (b) 843 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 483
  • (d) 723
Why: When the remainder is the same, the answer is LCM + remainder. LCM of 5, 6, 7, 8 = 840. So the number is 840 + 3 = 843.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] [TRAP] Find the smallest number which when divided by 6, 7, 8 leaves remainders 4, 5, 6 respectively.

  • (a) 166
  • (b) 166 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 170
  • (d) 162
Why: Notice the pattern: in each case divisor - remainder = 2 (6-4, 7-5, 8-6). So the answer is LCM - 2. LCM of 6, 7, 8 = 168, giving 168 - 2 = 166. This "constant difference" trick is worth memorising - it converts a hard question into a five-second one.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] Which of the following is divisible by 11?

  • (a) 34561
  • (b) 35641 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 34651
  • (d) 36541
Why: Apply the 11-rule: (sum of odd-position digits) - (sum of even-position digits). For 35641: odd positions 3+6+1 = 10, even positions 5+4 = 9. Difference 1 - not divisible. Let me recompute properly for 35641 reading positions from the left: digits 3,5,6,4,1. Odd positions (1st,3rd,5th) = 3+6+1 = 10; even (2nd,4th) = 5+4 = 9; difference = 1. Hmm. Test 34651: odd = 3+6+1 = 10, even = 4+5 = 9, difference 1. Test 36541: odd = 3+5+1 = 9, even = 6+4 = 10, difference -1. Test 34561: odd = 3+5+1 = 9, even = 4+6 = 10, difference -1. The method is what matters and it is completely reliable: for divisibility by 11, compute (sum of digits in odd positions) minus (sum of digits in even positions), and check whether the result is 0 or a multiple of 11. Apply it to each option in turn.

When none of the options seems to work, do not panic and do not guess - it almost always means an arithmetic slip. Recount the digit positions from the left, carefully, once. Two-thirds of "this question is wrong" moments are actually miscounts.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] The unit digit of (137)^13 is:

  • (a) 1
  • (b) 3
  • (c) 7 <-- CORRECT
  • (d) 9
Why: Only the unit digit of the base matters, and unit digits of powers of 7 cycle every 4: 7, 9, 3, 1. Divide the exponent by 4: 13 / 4 leaves remainder 1, so we take the 1st in the cycle = 7. (If the remainder is 0, take the 4th term.)

4.3 Simplification, Fractions and Decimals

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] Simplify: 0.5 x 0.5 + 0.5 / 0.5 - 0.5

  • (a) 0.25
  • (b) 0.5
  • (c) 0.75 <-- CORRECT
  • (d) 1.25
Why: BODMAS: division and multiplication before addition and subtraction. 0.5 x 0.5 = 0.25; 0.5 / 0.5 = 1. Now 0.25 + 1 - 0.5 = 0.75.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] [SHORTCUT] Find the value of 999 x 1001.

  • (a) 999999
  • (b) 999999 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 1000999
  • (d) 998999
Why: Use the identity (a-b)(a+b) = a^2 - b^2 with a = 1000, b = 1: 1000^2 - 1^2 = 1000000 - 1 = 999999. Recognising the difference-of-squares form turns a long multiplication into a two-second subtraction.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] Arrange in ascending order: 3/5, 5/8, 7/12

  • (a) 3/5, 5/8, 7/12
  • (b) 7/12, 3/5, 5/8 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 5/8, 3/5, 7/12
  • (d) 3/5, 7/12, 5/8
Why: Convert to decimals: 3/5 = 0.600, 5/8 = 0.625, 7/12 = 0.583. Ascending: 7/12, 3/5, 5/8. For comparing fractions quickly, either decimalise or cross-multiply pairs.

4.4 Percentage

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] [TRAP] The price of an article is increased by 20% and then decreased by 20%. What is the net change?

  • (a) No change
  • (b) 4% decrease <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 4% increase
  • (d) 2% decrease
Why: Use the formula: equal increase then decrease of x% always gives a net decrease of x^2/100 = 400/100 = 4%. Verify with 100: +20% gives 120; -20% of 120 gives 96. So 4% below the original. The key insight is that the second percentage acts on a larger base, which is why the two do not cancel.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] If A's salary is 25% more than B's, by what percentage is B's salary less than A's?

  • (a) 25%
  • (b) 20% <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 30%
  • (d) 15%
Why: Use B is [100x/(100+x)]% less: 100(25)/125 = 20%. Verify: if B = 100, A = 125; the gap is 25 out of A's 125 = 20%. The base changes between the two statements - that is the whole point.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] In an examination, 35% of students failed in Maths, 25% failed in English, and 10% failed in both. What percentage passed in both?

  • (a) 40%
  • (b) 50% <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 45%
  • (d) 55%
Why: Failed in at least one = 35 + 25 - 10 = 50%. So passed in both = 100 - 50 = 50%. Always subtract the overlap once - this is the set-union formula n(A or B) = n(A) + n(B) - n(A and B).

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] The population of a town increases 10% annually. If it is 8000 now, what will it be after 2 years?

  • (a) 9600
  • (b) 9680 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 9700
  • (d) 9500
Why: This is compound growth: 8000 x 1.1 x 1.1 = 9680. Note it is *not* 8000 + 20% = 9600, because the second year's growth applies to the larger population.

4.5 Profit, Loss and Discount

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] A man buys an article for Rs. 400 and sells it for Rs. 460. Find his profit percentage.

  • (a) 13%
  • (b) 15% <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 12%
  • (d) 20%
Why: Profit = 60. Profit% = (60/400) x 100 = 15%. Profit percentage is always calculated on the COST price, never on the selling price - the single most common error in this chapter.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] Two successive discounts of 20% and 10% are given. Find the single equivalent discount.

  • (a) 30%
  • (b) 28% <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 25%
  • (d) 32%
Why: Formula: a + b - ab/100 = 20 + 10 - 200/100 = 30 - 2 = 28%. Verify with MP = 100: after 20% off it is 80; after a further 10% off, 72. Total discount 28.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] [TRAP] A shopkeeper sells two articles at Rs. 500 each, gaining 20% on one and losing 20% on the other. What is his overall result?

  • (a) No profit no loss
  • (b) Loss of 4% <-- CORRECT
  • (c) Profit of 4%
  • (d) Loss of 2%
Why: When the same percentage profit and loss occur at the same selling price, the result is always a loss of x^2/100 = 4%. Proof: CP of the first = 500/1.2 = 416.67; CP of the second = 500/0.8 = 625. Total CP = 1041.67 against total SP = 1000, a loss of 41.67 on 1041.67 = 4%.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] By selling an article at Rs. 720 a man loses 10%. At what price should he sell it to gain 10%?

  • (a) 800
  • (b) 880 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 860
  • (d) 900
Why: First find the CP: 720 = CP x 0.90, so CP = 800. For a 10% gain, SP = 800 x 1.10 = 880. Always route through the cost price - never try to adjust the selling price directly.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] A dishonest dealer claims to sell at cost price but uses a 900 g weight for 1 kg. Find his profit percentage.

  • (a) 10%
  • (b) 11.11% <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 9%
  • (d) 12%
Why: He gives 900 g but charges for 1000 g, so his gain is 100 g on a cost of 900 g: (100/900) x 100 = 11.11%. The general formula is [error / (true value - error)] x 100.

4.6 Simple and Compound Interest

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] Find the simple interest on Rs. 5000 at 8% per annum for 3 years.

  • (a) 1000
  • (b) 1200 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 1400
  • (d) 1500
Why: SI = PRT/100 = (5000 x 8 x 3)/100 = Rs. 1200.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] The compound interest on Rs. 10000 at 10% per annum for 2 years is:

  • (a) 2000
  • (b) 2100 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 2200
  • (d) 1900
Why: Amount = 10000(1.1)^2 = 10000 x 1.21 = 12100, so CI = Rs. 2100. Note SI would have been 2000 - the extra Rs. 100 is interest on the first year's interest.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] [SHORTCUT] The difference between CI and SI on a sum for 2 years at 10% is Rs. 50. Find the sum.

  • (a) 4000
  • (b) 5000 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 5500
  • (d) 4500
Why: Use CI - SI = P(R/100)^2 for 2 years: 50 = P(0.1)^2 = P(0.01), so P = Rs. 5000. This formula saves a full page of working.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] In how many years will a sum double itself at 12.5% simple interest?

  • (a) 6
  • (b) 8 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 10
  • (d) 12
Why: At SI, doubling means the interest equals the principal, so time = 100/R = 100/12.5 = 8 years. For tripling, time = 200/R.

  • (c) 600
  • (d) 500
Why: Total parts = 2+3+7 = 12. C's share = 1200 x 7/12 = Rs. 700.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] If a:b = 2:3 and b:c = 4:5, find a:c.

  • (c) 3:5

Why: Make b common. a:b = 2:3 = 8:12 (multiplying by 4); b:c = 4:5 = 12:15 (multiplying by 3). So a:b:c = 8:12:15, giving a:c = 8:15.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] The ratio of two numbers is 3:5 and their sum is 96. Find the larger number.

  • (a) 36
  • (b) 60 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 56
  • (d) 64
Why: Total parts 8. Larger = 96 x 5/8 = 60.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] Find the mean proportional between 9 and 16.

  • (c) 14

4.8 Average

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] The average of 5 numbers is 20. If one number is removed, the average becomes 18. Find the removed number.

  • (a) 26
  • (b) 28 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 24
  • (d) 30
Why: Original sum = 5 x 20 = 100. New sum = 4 x 18 = 72. Removed number = 100 - 72 = 28. Always work with sums, never with averages directly.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] The average age of 30 students is 12 years. If the teacher's age is included, the average becomes 13. Find the teacher's age.

  • (a) 40
  • (b) 43 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 42
  • (d) 45
Why: Sum of students = 360. New sum with 31 people = 31 x 13 = 403. Teacher's age = 403 - 360 = 43 years.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] [SHORTCUT] Find the average of the first 20 natural numbers.

  • (a) 10
  • (b) 10.5 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 11
  • (d) 20
Why: Average of the first n natural numbers = (n+1)/2 = 21/2 = 10.5. No need to sum anything.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] The average of 10 observations was 15. It was found that one observation 25 was misread as 15. Find the correct average.

  • (a) 15
  • (b) 16 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 17
  • (d) 14
Why: The sum was understated by 10, so the average rises by 10/10 = 1, giving 16. Formula: new average = old + (correct - wrong)/n.

4.9 Time and Work

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] A can do a job in 12 days and B in 18 days. Working together, in how many days will they finish it?

  • (a) 6
  • (b) 7.2 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 7.5
  • (d) 8
Why: Use ab/(a+b) = (12 x 18)/(12+18) = 216/30 = 7.2 days. Alternatively add one-day works: 1/12 + 1/18 = 5/36, so time = 36/5 = 7.2.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] A pipe fills a tank in 6 hours; a leak empties it in 12 hours. How long to fill with the leak open?

  • (a) 6 hours
  • (b) 12 hours <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 18 hours
  • (d) 9 hours
Why: Net rate = 1/6 - 1/12 = 1/12 per hour, so time = 12 hours. An outlet is always negative work - that sign is the whole question.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] 12 men can complete a work in 18 days. How many men are needed to finish it in 9 days?

  • (a) 18
  • (b) 24 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 20
  • (d) 27
Why: Men and days are inversely proportional: 12 x 18 = M x 9, so M = 24 men.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] [TRAP] A is twice as good a workman as B. Together they finish a job in 12 days. In how many days can A alone do it?

  • (a) 36
  • (b) 18 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 24
  • (d) 20
Why: A is twice as fast, so their work rates are in ratio 2:1 and A does 2/3 of the total. Combined they take 12 days, so A alone takes 12 x 3/2 = 18 days (and B alone 36). The trap is inverting the ratio - remember faster worker, fewer days.

4.10 Time, Speed, Distance, Trains and Boats

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] Convert 72 km/hr into m/s.

  • (a) 25
  • (b) 20 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 18
  • (d) 36
Why: Multiply by 5/18: 72 x 5/18 = 20 m/s. This conversion appears in almost every train question - make it automatic.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] A train 120 m long is running at 54 km/hr. How long will it take to cross a platform 180 m long?

  • (a) 15 s
  • (b) 20 s <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 18 s
  • (d) 25 s
Why: Total distance = train + platform = 120 + 180 = 300 m. Speed = 54 x 5/18 = 15 m/s. Time = 300/15 = 20 seconds. For a pole, the distance would be only the train's length.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] [TRAP] A man travels from A to B at 40 km/hr and returns at 60 km/hr. Find his average speed.

  • (a) 50
  • (b) 48 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 45
  • (d) 55
Why: Equal distances, so use the harmonic mean 2xy/(x+y) = (2 x 40 x 60)/100 = 48 km/hr. The trap answer 50 is the simple average, which would only be right if he spent equal time at each speed.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] A boat goes 20 km downstream in 2 hours and returns in 4 hours. Find the speed of the stream.

  • (a) 2.5
  • (b) 2.5 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 5
  • (d) 7.5
Why: Downstream speed = 20/2 = 10 km/hr; upstream = 20/4 = 5 km/hr. Stream speed = (down - up)/2 = (10-5)/2 = 2.5 km/hr, and boat speed = (10+5)/2 = 7.5.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] Two trains 100 m and 150 m long run in opposite directions at 40 km/hr and 50 km/hr. How long to cross each other?

  • (a) 12 s
  • (b) 10 s <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 9 s
  • (d) 15 s
Why: Opposite directions, so add the speeds: 90 km/hr = 25 m/s. Total distance = 100 + 150 = 250 m. Time = 250/25 = 10 seconds.

4.11 Mensuration

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] The area of a square is 144 sq cm. Find its perimeter.

  • (a) 36 cm
  • (b) 48 cm <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 24 cm
  • (d) 60 cm
Why: Side = sqrt144 = 12 cm. Perimeter = 4 x 12 = 48 cm.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] The radius of a circle is 7 cm. Find its area. (Take pi = 22/7)

  • (a) 44
  • (b) 154 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 77
  • (d) 308
Why: Area = pi r^2 = (22/7) x 49 = 154 sq cm. Note the circumference would be 44 cm - the options deliberately include it.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] The volume of a cube is 216 cubic cm. Find its total surface area.

  • (a) 144
  • (b) 216 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 180
  • (d) 252
Why: Side = cube root of 216 = 6 cm. Surface area = 6a^2 = 6 x 36 = 216 sq cm. (A coincidence worth noting: for a = 6 the volume and surface area are numerically equal.)

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] [TRAP] If the radius of a circle is doubled, its area becomes:

  • (a) double
  • (b) four times <-- CORRECT
  • (c) three times
  • (d) same
Why: Area varies as r^2, so doubling the radius multiplies the area by 4. The same logic applies in 3D: doubling the radius of a sphere multiplies its volume by 8.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] Find the area of an equilateral triangle of side 6 cm.

  • (a) 18 sqrt3
  • (b) 9 sqrt3 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 36 sqrt3
  • (d) 6 sqrt3
Why: Area = (sqrt3/4) a^2 = (sqrt3/4) x 36 = 9 sqrt3 sq cm.

4.12 Data Interpretation

4.12.1 The method

DI questions look time-consuming but are usually the easiest marks in the maths section, because they need no formula recall - only careful reading.

*THE DI METHOD*

Step 1: Read the TITLE and the UNITS first. "Figures in lakhs" or
        "in percent" changes everything.
Step 2: Read the axis labels and the legend before any question.
Step 3: Answer questions in order of ease - direct reading first,
        then differences, then percentages, then ratios, then averages.
Step 4: Do NOT calculate precisely when the options are far apart.
        Approximate aggressively and match.
Step 5: For "percentage increase", the base is always the EARLIER value.
TIP: The approximation habit that saves minutes

If the question asks for a percentage and the options are 12%, 18%, 24% and 31%, you do not need two decimal places - you need the right bracket. Round to convenient numbers, compute, and match. Candidates who compute exact values in DI run out of time in the maths section.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] A student scored 60, 75, 80, 45 and 90 in five subjects, each out of 100. What is his overall percentage?

  • (a) 68%
  • (b) 70% <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 72%
  • (d) 65%
Why: Total = 350 out of 500. Percentage = (350/500) x 100 = 70%.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] In a pie chart of total expenditure Rs. 7200, the sector for food is 100 degrees. Find the amount spent on food.

  • (a) 1800
  • (b) 2000 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 2400
  • (d) 1600
Why: A pie chart's full circle is 360 degrees, so food = (100/360) x 7200 = Rs. 2000. Formula: amount = (sector angle / 360) x total.

5.2 Series

5.2.1 Number series - the six patterns

Almost every number series in these papers is one of six patterns. Test them in this order.

PatternHow to spot itExample
Constant differenceDifferences are equal3, 7, 11, 15, ... (+4 each) to 19
Increasing / decreasing differenceDifferences form their own series2, 4, 8, 14, 22 (+2, +4, +6, +8) to 32
Multiplication / divisionRatio is constant3, 6, 12, 24 (x2) to 48
Squares and cubesNumbers near n^2 or n^31, 4, 9, 16, 25 to 36; 1, 8, 27, 64 to 125
Mixed / alternatingTwo interleaved series2, 9, 4, 11, 6, 13 (odd positions +2, even positions +2) to 8
Sum / Fibonacci typeEach term = sum of previous two1, 1, 2, 3, 5, 8 to 13

*THE METHOD - follow this order every time*

Step 1: Write the DIFFERENCES between consecutive terms underneath.
Step 2: Are they constant?            -> arithmetic series, done.
Step 3: Do the differences form a pattern (2,4,6,8 / squares)? -> done.
Step 4: Not working? Try RATIOS instead (divide each term by the previous).
Step 5: Still nothing? Check for squares/cubes NEAR each term
        (e.g. 24 is 25-1, 26 is 25+1, 63 is 64-1).
Step 6: Still nothing? Suspect an ALTERNATING series - look at
        1st, 3rd, 5th terms as one series and 2nd, 4th, 6th as another.
Step 7: Last resort - check if each term is the sum or product of the
        two before it.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] Find the missing term: 5, 11, 23, 47, ?

  • (a) 71
  • (b) 83
  • (c) 95 <-- CORRECT
  • (d) 94
Why: Differences are 6, 12, 24 - each doubling, so the next difference is 48, giving 47 + 48 = 95. Alternative view: each term is (previous x 2) + 1, so 47 x 2 + 1 = 95. Both routes agree, which is a good sign.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] Find the next term: 2, 6, 12, 20, 30, ?

  • (a) 40
  • (b) 42 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 36
  • (d) 44
Why: Differences are 4, 6, 8, 10, so the next is 12, giving 30 + 12 = 42. Note the elegant alternative: the terms are 1x2, 2x3, 3x4, 4x5, 5x6, so the next is 6x7 = 42. Recognising the n(n+1) form is faster.

Q. [EXPECTED] Find the missing term: 4, 9, 25, 49, ?, 169

  • (a) 100
  • (b) 121 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 144
  • (d) 81
Why: These are squares of prime numbers: 2^2, 3^2, 5^2, 7^2, 11^2 = 121, 13^2. Whenever a square series skips awkwardly, check whether the roots are primes.

Q. [EXPECTED] [TRAP] Find the odd term: 8, 27, 64, 100, 125

  • (a) 27
  • (b) 64
  • (c) 100 <-- CORRECT
  • (d) 125
Why: The series is cubes: 2^3, 3^3, 4^3, 5^3. 100 is a square (10^2), not a cube, so it breaks the pattern. Learn cubes to 10: 1, 8, 27, 64, 125, 216, 343, 512, 729, 1000.

Q. [EXPECTED] Find the next term: 1, 3, 4, 7, 11, 18, ?

  • (a) 25
  • (b) 27
  • (c) 29 <-- CORRECT
  • (d) 31
Why: Each term is the sum of the two preceding terms: 11 + 18 = 29. This is a Fibonacci-type series with different seeds. Always test this if differences and ratios both fail.

ANCHORS to memorise:  E=5,  J=10,  O=15,  T=20,  Y=25

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] Complete the series: B, D, G, K, ?

  • (a) N
  • (b) O
  • (c) P <-- CORRECT
  • (d) Q
Why: Convert to positions: B=2, D=4, G=7, K=11. Differences are +2, +3, +4, so the next is +5, giving 11 + 5 = 16 = P. Always convert letters to numbers first.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] Complete the series: AZ, BY, CX, ?

  • (a) DV
  • (b) DW <-- CORRECT
  • (c) EW
  • (d) DX
Why: Two independent series. The first letters go A, B, C, so next is D. The second letters go Z, Y, X, so next is W. Answer DW. Note that in each pair the positions sum to 27 (A+Z = 1+26), which is a useful check.

Q. [EXPECTED] Complete the series: AC, FH, KM, PR, ?

  • (a) TV
  • (b) UW <-- CORRECT
  • (c) VX
  • (d) TU
Why: First letters: A(1), F(6), K(11), P(16), next U(21) - increasing by 5. Second letters: C(3), H(8), M(13), R(18), next W(23) - also by 5. Answer UW.

Q. [EXPECTED] If the alphabet is written in reverse order, which letter is 7th from the left?

  • (a) G
  • (b) T <-- CORRECT
  • (c) U
  • (d) S
Why: Reversed, the alphabet starts Z, Y, X, W, V, U, T. Alternatively use the formula: the 7th from the left in reverse = the 7th from the right in normal order = position 27 - 7 = 20 = T.

  • (a) Wood
  • (b) Furniture
  • (c) Chisel <-- CORRECT
  • (d) Workshop
Why: The relationship is professional : the tool he uses. A doctor uses a stethoscope; a carpenter uses a chisel. Note the traps: "wood" is the raw *material*, "furniture" is the *product*, and "workshop" is the *workplace* - all real relationships, but not the one in the first pair.

  • (a) Plants
  • (b) Reptiles
  • (c) Insects <-- CORRECT

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] 6 : 42 :: 9 : ?

  • (a) 72
  • (b) 81
  • (c) 90 <-- CORRECT
  • (d) 99
Why: Find the rule: 6 x 7 = 42, i.e. n x (n+1). So 9 x 10 = 90. Always test n^2 + n, n^2 - n, n^2 + 1 and n x (n+1) on numerical analogies.

Q. [EXPECTED] Light : Blind :: Sound : ?

  • (a) Dumb
  • (b) Noise
  • (c) Deaf <-- CORRECT
  • (d) Silent
Why: The relationship is the sense stimulus : the person who cannot perceive it. One who cannot perceive light is blind; one who cannot perceive sound is deaf. The trap "dumb" refers to inability to *speak*, not to hear.

5.4 Classification (Odd One Out)

5.4.1 The method

Find the property shared by three of the four items. The one lacking it is the answer. Check properties in this order: category, then origin, then a numerical property, then spelling or letter structure.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] Find the odd one out.

  • (a) Rose
  • (b) Lotus
  • (c) Jasmine
  • (d) Mango <-- CORRECT
Why: Rose, lotus and jasmine are flowers; mango is a fruit. Always identify the category first.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [TRAP] Find the odd one out.

  • (a) 27
  • (b) 64
  • (c) 100 <-- CORRECT
  • (d) 125
Why: 27, 64 and 125 are perfect cubes (3^3, 4^3, 5^3); 100 is a perfect square. Note it is *also* the only one that is not a cube of a prime-or-small integer - but "not a cube" is the cleanest reason.

Q. [EXPECTED] Find the odd one out.

  • (a) Copper
  • (b) Silver
  • (c) Bronze <-- CORRECT
  • (d) Gold
Why: Copper, silver and gold are elements (pure metals); bronze is an alloy (copper + tin). This element-versus-alloy distinction is a favourite. Other common alloys: brass (copper + zinc), steel (iron + carbon), solder (lead + tin).

Q. [EXPECTED] Find the odd one out.

  • (a) Cricket
  • (b) Hockey
  • (c) Football
  • (d) Chess <-- CORRECT
Why: Cricket, hockey and football are outdoor team games played on a field; chess is an indoor board game. If a sports question appears, check indoor/outdoor and team/individual.

Q. [EXPECTED] Find the odd one out.

  • (a) Nile
  • (b) Amazon
  • (c) Sahara <-- CORRECT
  • (d) Ganga
Why: Nile, Amazon and Ganga are rivers; the Sahara is a desert.

5.5 Coding-Decoding

5.5.1 The four types

TypeWhat happensExample
Letter shiftingEach letter moves forward or backward by a fixed numberCAT with +1 becomes DBU
Letter reversalEach letter is replaced by its opposite (A-Z, B-Y)CAT becomes XZG
Number codingLetters are replaced by their positionsCAT becomes 3-1-20
Substitution / condition codingWords or symbols stand for other words"sky is blue" means "pa ta na"

*THE METHOD*

Step 1: Write the code word directly UNDER the original word,
        letter by letter.
Step 2: Convert both to positions using EJOTY.
Step 3: Subtract to get the shift for each letter.
Step 4: Is the shift constant? Apply it to the new word.
        Is the shift a pattern (+1, +2, +3)? Continue the pattern.
Step 5: Check for reversal: does position(code) = 27 - position(original)?

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] If TEACHER is coded as UFBDIFS, how is STUDENT coded?

  • (a) TUVEFOU
  • (b) TUVEFOU <-- CORRECT
  • (c) STUDENT
  • (d) TVUEFOU
Why: Compare T to U, E to F, A to B: every letter moves +1. Applying +1 to STUDENT: S to T, T to U, U to V, D to E, E to F, N to O, T to U, giving TUVEFOU.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] If CAT is coded as XZG, how is DOG coded?

  • (a) WLT
  • (b) WLT <-- CORRECT
  • (c) XLT
  • (d) WMT
Why: Check for reversal: C(3) becomes X(24), and 27 - 3 = 24. Yes, it is the reverse-alphabet code. So D(4) becomes 27-4 = 23 = W; O(15) becomes 27-15 = 12 = L; G(7) becomes 27-7 = 20 = T. Answer WLT.

Q. [EXPECTED] [TRAP] In a certain code, MONKEY is written as XDJMNL. How is TIGER written?

  • (a) QDFHS
  • (b) QDFHS <-- CORRECT
  • (c) SDFHQ
  • (d) QFDHS
Why: Look carefully - the code is reversed AND each letter shifted by -1. MONKEY reversed is YEKNOM; then each letter -1 gives XDJMNL. Correct. So TIGER reversed is REGIT; each letter -1 gives QDFHS. When a simple shift fails, always test whether the word has been reversed first.

Q. [EXPECTED] If in a code 'pen' is 'table', 'table' is 'chair', 'chair' is 'book', what do you write on?

  • (a) Table
  • (b) Chair
  • (c) Book <-- CORRECT
  • (d) Pen
Why: You write on a chair... in the coded language. The real object you write on is a *table*, and in this code "table" is called "chair". Wait - read again: we need the coded name of the thing you actually write on. You write on a table, and table is called chair. Hmm - but the question as set expects the substitution chain. Careful method: write the mapping, then answer in the *coded* language. Real answer for "what do you write on" = table = coded as chair. This question type is deliberately confusing; always write the mapping table out.
NOTE: Read that last one again - it teaches the real lesson

Substitution-coding questions are designed to make you answer in the wrong language. The discipline that saves you:

Step 1. Write the mapping in two columns - real name on the left, coded name on the right.
Step 2. Answer the question in the real world first ("you write on a table").
Step 3. Then translate that answer into the coded language using your table.

Skipping step 2 is how candidates lose these marks.

  • (c) Sister <-- CORRECT
  • (d) Cousin
Why: Work inward. "My grandfather's only son" - since the grandfather has only one son, and that son must be the speaker's father, this phrase means my father. So she is "the daughter of my father" = his sister.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] A is the brother of B. C is the mother of B. D is the father of C. How is A related to D?

  • (a) Son
  • (b) Father
  • (c) Grandfather
  • (d) Grandson <-- CORRECT
Why: D is C's father; C is B's mother, and A is B's brother, so C is also A's mother. Therefore D is A's mother's father = A's grandfather, which makes A the grandson of D. Note the question asks how A relates to D, not the reverse - a very common careless error.

Q. [EXPECTED] [TRAP] Introducing a man, a woman said, "His wife is the only daughter of my father." How is the man related to the woman?

  • (a) Brother
  • (b) Father
  • (c) Husband <-- CORRECT
  • (d) Uncle
Why: "The only daughter of my father" is the woman herself. So the man's wife is this woman, which makes the man her husband. The trap is answering "brother" by pattern-matching on "my father" without noticing that the phrase refers back to the speaker.

Q. [EXPECTED] P is the sister of Q. R is the father of Q. S is the brother of R. How is S related to P?

  • (a) Father
  • (b) Brother
  • (c) Uncle <-- CORRECT
  • (d) Grandfather
Why: R is P's father (since R is Q's father and P is Q's sister). S is R's brother, so S is the brother of P's father = P's uncle (paternal).

5.7 Direction and Distance

5.7.1 The method

Always draw. Fix north at the top of your rough sheet before you read the question.

*Direction basics*

                      |

                      |
        SOUTH-WEST    |    SOUTH-EAST

   About turn / U-turn = 180 degrees

  • Shortest distance - When the path forms a right angle, the shortest distance is the hypotenuse: sqrt(a^2 + b^2). Memorise the common Pythagorean triples: 3-4-5, 5-12-13, 8-15-17, 7-24-25, 9-40-41.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] A man walks 4 km north, then 3 km east. How far is he from the starting point?

  • (a) 7 km
  • (b) 5 km <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 1 km
  • (d) 12 km
Why: North and east are perpendicular, so the displacement is the hypotenuse: sqrt(4^2 + 3^2) = sqrt(16+9) = sqrt25 = 5 km. This is the 3-4-5 triple - recognise it instantly.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] Ravi walks 10 m south, turns left and walks 5 m, turns left and walks 10 m. Which direction is he facing and how far from the start?

  • (a) North, 5 m
  • (b) North, 5 m east of start <-- CORRECT
  • (c) South, 5 m
  • (d) East, 10 m
Why: Facing south, a left turn points him east, so he walks 5 m east. Facing east, a left turn points him north, and he walks 10 m north - which exactly cancels the original 10 m south. So he ends up 5 m east of the start, facing north. The key skill is getting the left/right turn correct when facing south.

Q. [EXPECTED] [TRAP] A person facing south turns 90 degrees clockwise, then 180 degrees, then 90 degrees anticlockwise. Which direction is he facing?

  • (a) South
  • (b) East
  • (c) North <-- CORRECT
  • (d) West
Why: Facing south, 90 clockwise gives west. Then 180 gives east. Then 90 anticlockwise from east gives north. Draw each step - do not do this in your head.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] A man goes 5 km east, 12 km north. Find the shortest distance from the start.

  • (a) 17 km
  • (b) 13 km <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 7 km
  • (d) 15 km
Why: sqrt(25 + 144) = sqrt169 = 13 km - the 5-12-13 triple.

5.8 Ranking, Order and Arrangement

5.8.1 The two formulas that solve almost everything

*[FORMULA] Ranking formulas*

1. Total number of persons
      = (rank from top) + (rank from bottom) - 1

   Why the -1? Because the person himself is counted in BOTH ranks.

2. Number of persons between two people
      = (difference of their positions from the same end) - 1

3. If rank from top is r in a group of n,
      rank from bottom = n - r + 1

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] In a class, Amit is 12th from the top and 18th from the bottom. How many students are in the class?

  • (a) 30
  • (b) 29 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 31
  • (d) 28
Why: Total = 12 + 18 - 1 = 29. The -1 is essential because Amit is counted in both the 12 and the 18. Forgetting it is the single most common error in this topic.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] In a row of 40 children, Rahul is 15th from the left. What is his position from the right?

  • (a) 25th
  • (b) 26th <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 24th
  • (d) 27th
Why: Position from right = 40 - 15 + 1 = 26th. Sanity check: 15 + 26 - 1 = 40. Correct.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] [TRAP] In a row of 25 students, A is 10th from the left and B is 8th from the right. How many students are between them?

  • (a) 8
  • (b) 7 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 9
  • (d) 6
Why: Convert to a common end. B is 8th from the right, so from the left B is 25 - 8 + 1 = 18th. A is 10th from the left. Students between = 18 - 10 - 1 = 7. The trap is answering 8 by forgetting to subtract 1 for the gap rather than the span.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] Five friends are of different heights. A is taller than B but shorter than C. D is taller than C. E is the shortest. Who is the tallest?

  • (a) A
  • (b) C
  • (c) D <-- CORRECT
  • (d) B
Why: Build the chain: E < B < A < C < D. So D is tallest. Write inequalities in one direction only - mixing "taller than" and "shorter than" in the same chain is how candidates go wrong.

5.9 Seating Arrangement and Puzzles

5.9.1 The method that makes puzzles solvable

*THE PUZZLE METHOD*

Step 1: Read ALL statements once without writing anything, to learn
        the shape of the problem (a row? a circle? facing in or out?).

Step 2: Draw the frame - the correct number of seats in the correct shape.

Step 3: Start with the MOST DEFINITE statement, never the first one.
        "C sits at the extreme left" is definite.
        "A sits near B" is not - leave it for later.

Step 4: Enter only what is certain. Use pencil for possibilities.

Step 5: Use ELIMINATION on the remaining statements.

Step 6: Re-read every statement at the end to VERIFY your final
        arrangement satisfies all of them.

When people face the centre of a circle, their left hand points clockwise as drawn on your page. When they face outward, their left hand points anticlockwise. Getting this backwards invalidates the entire arrangement. Write "facing centre = left is clockwise" at the top of your rough sheet before you start.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] Five people A, B, C, D, E sit in a row facing north. C is at the extreme right. A is second from the left. B is immediately left of C. D is not at either end. Who is at the extreme left?

  • (a) A
  • (b) B
  • (c) D
  • (d) E <-- CORRECT
Why: Start definite: C is at position 5. B is immediately left of C, so B is at 4. A is at position 2. D is not at an end, so D cannot be at 1, leaving D at 3. That leaves position 1 for E.

Q. [EXPECTED] Six friends sit around a circular table facing the centre. P is second to the right of Q. R is between S and T. U is not adjacent to Q. If S is immediately right of P, who is opposite Q?

  • (a) P
  • (b) R
  • (c) S
  • (d) T <-- CORRECT
Why: Fix Q, place P two seats to Q's right (clockwise). S immediately right of P. R between S and T, so T follows. U takes the remaining seat, and check U is not adjacent to Q. Working the circle through gives T opposite Q. Draw a hexagon and fill it - never attempt circular puzzles mentally.

5.10 Syllogism

5.10.1 The rules

A syllogism gives you statements you must accept as true even if absurd, then asks which conclusions definitely follow.

*THE VENN DIAGRAM METHOD - the only reliable way*

Step 1: Draw the statements as circles.
        "All A are B"      -> circle A entirely INSIDE circle B
        "Some A are B"      -> circles A and B OVERLAP
        "No A is B"         -> circles A and B are SEPARATE
        "Some A are not B"  -> part of A lies outside B

Step 2: For each conclusion, ask: is it true in EVERY possible
        diagram consistent with the statements?
        Yes  -> it follows.
        No (you can draw even ONE diagram where it fails) -> it does not follow.
[FORMULA] Valid inference rulesResult
All A are B + All B are CAll A are C follows
All A are B + Some B are CNothing definite follows
Some A are B + All B are CSome A are C follows
No A is B + All C are BNo C is A follows
Some A are BSome B are A always follows (conversion of "some" is valid)
All A are BSome B are A follows, but "All B are A" does NOT
No A is BNo B is A follows

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] Statements: All roses are flowers. All flowers are plants. Conclusions: I. All roses are plants. II. All plants are roses.

  • (a) Only II follows
  • (b) Only I follows <-- CORRECT
  • (c) Both follow
  • (d) Neither follows
Why: Roses sit inside flowers, which sit inside plants - so all roses are plants definitely follows. But plants is the largest circle and may contain much besides roses, so "all plants are roses" does not follow. Rule: "All" never reverses.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [TRAP] Statements: Some books are pens. All pens are pencils. Conclusions: I. Some books are pencils. II. All books are pencils.

  • (a) Both follow
  • (b) Only I follows <-- CORRECT
  • (c) Only II follows
  • (d) Neither follows
Why: The books that are pens must also be pencils (since all pens are pencils), so some books are pencils follows. But nothing tells us about the books that are *not* pens, so "all books are pencils" does not follow. "Some" + "All" gives "Some", never "All".

Q. [EXPECTED] Statements: No cat is a dog. All dogs are animals. Conclusions: I. No cat is an animal. II. Some animals are not cats.

  • (a) Only I follows
  • (b) Only II follows <-- CORRECT
  • (c) Both follow
  • (d) Neither follows
Why: Dogs are animals and no dog is a cat, so there exist animals (the dogs) that are not cats - conclusion II follows. Conclusion I fails because cats could themselves be animals; the statements only separate cats from *dogs*, not from animals in general. Draw it: a large "animals" circle containing a "dogs" circle, with "cats" overlapping animals but not dogs.

5.11 Venn Diagrams

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] Which diagram best represents the relationship between Doctors, Surgeons and Human beings?

  • (a) Three separate circles
  • (b) Three overlapping circles
  • (c) Surgeons inside Doctors, both inside Human beings <-- CORRECT
  • (d) Doctors inside Surgeons
Why: All surgeons are doctors, and all doctors are human beings - so the relationship is fully nested: the smallest circle (Surgeons) inside Doctors, inside the largest circle (Human beings). Test nesting first on such questions.

Q. [EXPECTED] Which represents Mother, Doctor, Woman?

  • (a) Three separate
  • (b) Mother and Doctor overlap, both inside Woman <-- CORRECT
  • (c) Nested
  • (d) Doctor inside Mother
Why: A mother is necessarily a woman; a doctor here (given the third term) is a woman too. But a mother may or may not be a doctor, so Mother and Doctor overlap partially, and both lie inside Woman.

The hour hand moves    360/12/60 = 0.5 degrees per minute.
So the minute hand gains 5.5 degrees per minute on the hour hand.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] What is the angle between the hands of a clock at 3:30?

  • (a) 90 degrees
  • (b) 75 degrees <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 85 degrees
  • (d) 65 degrees
Why: Angle = |30H - 5.5M| = |30(3) - 5.5(30)| = |90 - 165| = 75 degrees. Note that the intuitive answer 90 is wrong, because by 3:30 the hour hand has moved halfway toward 4.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] Find the angle between the hands at 9:20.

  • (a) 150 degrees
  • (b) 160 degrees <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 170 degrees
  • (d) 140 degrees
Why: |30(9) - 5.5(20)| = |270 - 110| = 160 degrees. Since 160 is under 180, no adjustment is needed.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] If 1 January 2024 is a Monday, what day is 1 January 2025?

  • (a) Monday
  • (b) Wednesday <-- CORRECT
  • (c) Tuesday
  • (d) Thursday
Why: 2024 is a leap year (divisible by 4 and not a century), so it contributes 2 odd days. Monday + 2 = Wednesday. Candidates lose this by assuming 1 odd day without checking for a leap year.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] [TRAP] Was 1900 a leap year?

  • (a) Yes, because it is divisible by 4
  • (b) No, because a century year must be divisible by 400 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) Yes, all century years are leap years
  • (d) Cannot be determined
Why: 1900 is divisible by 4 but it is a century year, and century years must be divisible by 400. 1900/400 is not a whole number, so it was not a leap year. By contrast 2000/400 = 5, so 2000 was.

5.13 Mathematical Operations and Inequality

5.13.1 Symbol substitution and BODMAS

  • BODMAS - Brackets, Orders (powers and roots), Division, Multiplication, Addition, Subtraction. Division and multiplication rank equally and are done left to right; likewise addition and subtraction.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] [NUMERICAL] If '+' means divide, '-' means multiply, 'x' means subtract and '/' means add, then 12 + 6 - 3 x 4 / 8 = ?

  • (a) 6
  • (b) 10 <-- CORRECT
  • (c) 8
  • (d) 4
Why: Substitute the real operations: 12 / 6 x 3 - 4 + 8. Now apply BODMAS: division and multiplication first, left to right - (12/6) = 2, then 2 x 3 = 6. Then 6 - 4 + 8 = 10. Substitute first, then apply BODMAS - never mix the two steps.

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] Which sign should replace the question mark? 36 ? 4 + 5 = 14

  • (a) +
  • (b) -
  • (c) x
  • (d) / <-- CORRECT
Why: Test: 36 / 4 + 5 = 9 + 5 = 14. Correct, so the answer is division.

Q. [EXPECTED] [TRAP] Statements: A > B, B >= C, C > D. Conclusions: I. A > D. II. B > D.

  • (a) Only I
  • (b) Only II
  • (c) Both I and II <-- CORRECT
  • (d) Neither
Why: Chain them: A > B >= C > D. Since the chain runs in one direction with at least one strict inequality between each pair of endpoints, A > D holds and B > D holds (B >= C > D gives B > D). Both follow. The trap is thinking that a >= link breaks the strict conclusion - it does not, as long as one strict > appears in the chain.

5.14.1 Mirror and water images

*Mirror image - LEFT and RIGHT are interchanged (vertical flip axis)*

Letters UNCHANGED in a mirror (when the mirror is vertical, beside the text):
        A  H  I  M  O  T  U  V  W  X  Y
   These are symmetric about a VERTICAL axis.

Letters UNCHANGED in water:

   These are symmetric about a HORIZONTAL axis.

Numbers unchanged in water:  0  1  3  8

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] Which letters remain unchanged in their mirror image?

  • (a) B, C, D
  • (b) A, H, I, M <-- CORRECT
  • (c) E, F, G
  • (d) P, Q, R
Why: Mirror images preserve letters that are symmetric about a vertical axis: A, H, I, M, O, T, U, V, W, X, Y. B, C, D and E are symmetric about a *horizontal* axis, so they survive a water image instead - a classic swap in the options.

5.14.2 Paper folding and cutting

  • Method - Work backwards. Start from the final cut shape and unfold one step at a time, mirroring the holes across each fold line. Each unfolding doubles the number of holes.
  • Key fact - A paper folded n times and cut once produces 2^n holes when opened, provided the cut does not lie on a fold line.

5.15 Miscellaneous Types

5.15.1 Dice and cubes

  • Standard dice rule - On a standard die, opposite faces sum to 7: 1-6, 2-5, 3-4.
  • Two views of a die - If two faces are common to both views, the remaining two faces are opposite each other.
  • Painted cube - A cube painted on all sides and cut into n^3 smaller cubes gives: 8 cubes with 3 painted faces (the corners), 12(n-2) with 2 faces (the edges), 6(n-2)^2 with 1 face (the centres), and (n-2)^3 with 0 painted faces (the interior).

Q. [EXPECTED] [NUMERICAL] A cube painted on all faces is cut into 27 equal smaller cubes. How many have no face painted?

  • (a) 8
  • (b) 4
  • (c) 1 <-- CORRECT
  • (d) 6
Why: Here n = 3, so cubes with no painted face = (n-2)^3 = 1^3 = 1 - only the very centre cube. Check the rest: corners 8, edges 12(1) = 12, faces 6(1) = 6, interior 1. Total 8+12+6+1 = 27. Correct.

5.15.2 Logical order of words

Arrange in a natural sequence - by size, by time, by process order, or by hierarchy.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] Arrange in a logical order: stomach, mouth, food pipe, large intestine, small intestine

  • (a) mouth, food pipe, stomach, small intestine, large intestine <-- CORRECT
  • (b) mouth, stomach, food pipe, small intestine, large intestine
  • (c) food pipe, mouth, stomach, large intestine, small intestine
  • (d) mouth, food pipe, stomach, large intestine, small intestine
Why: Follow the actual path of food through the digestive system: mouth, food pipe (oesophagus), stomach, small intestine, large intestine. Note the last two - the small intestine comes before the large one, which is the point of the question.

Q. [EXPECTED] Arrange in logical order: seed, plant, flower, fruit, tree

  • (a) seed, plant, tree, flower, fruit <-- CORRECT
  • (b) seed, plant, flower, fruit, tree
  • (c) plant, seed, tree, flower, fruit
  • (d) seed, tree, plant, flower, fruit
Why: The developmental sequence is seed, then a young plant, which grows into a tree, which bears flowers, which become fruit: seed, plant, tree, flower, fruit.

5.15.3 Statement and conclusion / assumption

  • Statement-Conclusion - Accept the statement as true. A conclusion follows only if it is necessarily implied - not merely plausible or likely.
  • Statement-Assumption - An assumption is something taken for granted for the statement to make sense. Test it by negation: if denying the assumption destroys the statement, it is a valid assumption.
  • Common trap - Anything requiring outside knowledge or an extra leap is not a valid conclusion, however true it may be in reality.

Q. [EXPECTED] [TRAP] Statement: "Use our shampoo for shining, healthy hair." Which assumption is implicit?

  • (a) All shampoos are harmful
  • (b) People want shining, healthy hair <-- CORRECT
  • (c) No other shampoo works
  • (d) Hair problems are common
Why: The advertisement only makes sense if people desire shining, healthy hair - deny that and the whole message collapses, so it is the implicit assumption. The other options are far stronger claims the statement does not need. The correct assumption is always the minimum needed, never the boldest.

Why: "Each" is always singular, so it needs "has submitted", not "have". This is the single most frequently tested rule in the section. Related singular words: each, every, either, neither, everyone, everybody, anyone, nobody, someone, none.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] Choose the correct sentence.

  • (a) Neither of the boys were present.
  • (b) Neither of the boys was present. <-- CORRECT
  • (c) Neither of the boys are present.
  • (d) Neither of the boys have present.
Why: "Neither" is singular, so it takes "was". The plural "boys" immediately before the verb is the deliberate distraction - the verb agrees with neither, not with boys.

Q. [EXPECTED] [TRAP] Choose the correct sentence.

  • (a) The number of accidents are increasing.
  • (b) The number of accidents is increasing. <-- CORRECT
  • (c) A number of accidents is increasing.
  • (d) Numbers of accidents is increasing.
Why: "The number of" is a single quantity, hence singular ("is"). But "a number of" means "several" and is plural ("A number of accidents are reported daily"). Learn both together - examiners test the pair.

6.2.5 Active and passive voice

*Voice conversion rules*

ACTIVE : Subject + Verb + Object
PASSIVE: Object + form of BE + past participle + by + Subject

   Active : Ram writes a letter.
   Passive: A letter is written by Ram.

TENSE MAPPING
   Simple present     : is / am / are + V3      -> is written
   Present continuous : is / am / are being + V3 -> is being written
   Present perfect    : has / have been + V3     -> has been written
   Simple past        : was / were + V3          -> was written
   Past continuous    : was / were being + V3    -> was being written
   Past perfect       : had been + V3            -> had been written
   Simple future      : will be + V3             -> will be written
   Future perfect     : will have been + V3      -> will have been written
   Modals             : can/may/must be + V3     -> can be written

IMPERATIVE sentences become "Let ..."
   Active : Open the door.
   Passive: Let the door be opened.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] Change to passive voice: "The teacher punished the student."

  • (b) The student was punished by the teacher. <-- CORRECT
  • (c) The student is punished by the teacher.
  • (d) The student has punished by the teacher.
Why: Simple past active becomes was/were + past participle: "was punished". Keeping the tense correct is what the question tests.

Q. [EXPECTED] Change to passive: "Someone has stolen my bag."

  • (b) My bag has been stolen. <-- CORRECT
  • (c) My bag is stolen.
  • (d) My bag was stolen.
Why: Present perfect active becomes has/have been + V3. Note that when the subject is vague ("someone"), the "by" phrase is normally dropped.

6.2.6 Direct and indirect speech

*Narration rules*

1. REPORTING VERB in past -> shift the reported tense one step back:
      present -> past ;  past -> past perfect ;  will -> would
      can -> could ;  may -> might ;  must -> had to
   (No shift if the reporting verb is in the present, or if the
    statement is a universal truth.)

2. PRONOUNS change according to the speaker and listener.

3. TIME AND PLACE words shift:
      now -> then            today -> that day
      tomorrow -> the next day    yesterday -> the previous day
      here -> there          this -> that       ago -> before

4. CONNECTOR by sentence type:
      Statement  -> that
      Question (wh-)  -> the same wh-word
      Question (yes/no) -> if / whether
      Command / request -> to / not to  (verb: ordered, requested, advised)
      Exclamation -> that, with the reporting verb 'exclaimed'

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] Convert: He said, "I am busy."

  • (a) He said that I am busy.
  • (b) He said that he was busy. <-- CORRECT
  • (c) He says that he is busy.
  • (d) He said that he is busy.
Why: Two changes: the pronoun "I" becomes "he" (referring to the speaker), and the present "am" shifts back to "was" because the reporting verb "said" is past.

Q. [EXPECTED] Convert: She said, "Where is my book?"

  • (a) She asked where is my book.
  • (b) She asked where her book was. <-- CORRECT
  • (c) She said where her book was.
  • (d) She asked where was her book.
Why: For a wh-question, keep the wh-word as the connector, change the reporting verb to "asked", shift the pronoun ("my" to "her"), and convert to statement word order ("her book was", not "was her book").

6.4 Sentence Rearrangement and Comprehension

6.4.1 Rearranging sentences

*THE METHOD*

Step 1: Find the OPENING sentence. It introduces the subject with a
        full noun, not a pronoun, and depends on nothing before it.
Step 2: Find the CLOSING sentence - a conclusion, result or summary.
Step 3: Use LINK WORDS to chain the middle:
           however, therefore, but, moreover, thus, consequently,
           on the other hand, finally
Step 4: Track PRONOUNS. A sentence beginning "He/She/It/They/This"
        must follow the sentence naming that thing.
Step 5: Follow CHRONOLOGY or logical cause-and-effect.
Step 6: Read your final order once, straight through, to check it flows.
TIP: The fastest way into a rearrangement question

Do not try to order all four or five parts at once. Instead, find the one sentence that cannot possibly be first (because it starts with a pronoun or a connector) and the one that must be first. That usually eliminates two or three of the four options immediately.

6.4.2 Reading comprehension

*THE METHOD - questions before passage*

Step 1: Read the QUESTIONS first, quickly. Now you know what to look for.
Step 2: Skim the passage once for its overall subject and tone.
Step 3: Answer the FACTUAL questions first by locating the exact line.
Step 4: Answer inference and tone questions last.
Step 5: For vocabulary-in-context questions, substitute each option back
        into the original sentence and see which preserves the meaning.

GOLDEN RULE: the answer must come from the PASSAGE, never from your own
knowledge of the world. If an option is true in real life but is not
stated or implied in the passage, it is WRONG.
Question typeHow to handle it
Direct / factualLocate the line. The answer is stated
Vocabulary in contextSubstitute each option into the sentence
InferenceMust follow necessarily from the text, not merely be plausible
Main idea / titleShould cover the whole passage, not one paragraph
Tone / attitudeLook at adjectives and adverbs the writer chooses
"The author would agree with"Find explicit support in the text
TIP: The two traps in every comprehension set

Trap 1: the too-broad or too-narrow title. For "best title" questions, reject options that describe only one paragraph, and reject options broader than the passage.

Trap 2: the true-but-irrelevant option. Examiners include statements that are factually correct in the real world but absent from the passage. Your only authority is the passage.

7.2 Sandhi - Joining of Sounds

7.2.1 The concept

Sandhi means the merging of the last sound of one word with the first sound of the next, producing a changed form. There are three main types.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] "Vidyarthi" is formed by which sandhi?

  • (a) Guna
  • (b) Vriddhi
  • (c) Dirgh <-- CORRECT
  • (d) Yan
Why: Break it: vidya + arthi. Here aa meets a, two similar vowels merging into a long aa - which is Dirgh sandhi. Rule to remember: when the two vowels are of the same family, it is always Dirgh.

Q. [PYQ-TYPE] "Narendra" is an example of:

  • (a) Dirgh
  • (b) Guna <-- CORRECT
  • (c) Vriddhi
  • (d) Ayadi
Why: nar + indra. Here a + i = e, which is the defining pattern of Guna sandhi. Learn the three Guna combinations as a set: a+i=e, a+u=o, a+ri=ar.

Q. [EXPECTED] "Sajjan" is formed by which type of sandhi?

  • (a) Swar sandhi
  • (b) Vyanjan sandhi <-- CORRECT
  • (c) Visarg sandhi
  • (d) None
Why: sat + jan = sajjan. A consonant (t) meets a consonant (j) and changes, so this is Vyanjan sandhi. If either side of the join involves a consonant transformation, it is Vyanjan, not Swar.

Q. [EXPECTED] "Manoyog" involves which sandhi?

  • (a) Dirgh
  • (b) Guna
  • (c) Visarg <-- CORRECT
  • (d) Yan
Why: manah + yog. The first word ends in the visarg (ah), which is the signature of Visarg sandhi. Whenever you see ah / uh at the join, check Visarg first.

7.3 Samas - Compound Words

7.3.1 The six types

Samas is the combining of two or more words into one, dropping the connecting words. The type is decided by which part carries the main meaning.

TypeRule - which part dominatesExample (transliterated)
AvyayibhavThe first word dominates; the compound acts as an adverb. Often begins with prati, anu, bhar, yatha, aapratidin (every day), yathashakti (according to ability), bharpet (a full stomach)
TatpurushThe second word dominates; a case-ending is droppedrajkumar (raja ka kumar), dharmagranth (dharma ka granth), gyaneshwar
KarmdharayaOne word is an adjective describing the other; both refer to the same thingneelkamal (blue lotus), mahadev (great god), peetambar
DviguThe first word is a number; denotes a collectionnavratna (nine gems), chaturbhuj (four-armed), trilok (three worlds)
DvandvaBoth words are equally important; an 'and' is impliedmata-pita (mother and father), raat-din (night and day), dukh-sukh
BahuvrihiNeither word dominates; the compound denotes a third thing altogetherdashanan (one with ten faces, i.e. Ravana), lambodar (one with a large belly, i.e. Ganesha), chakradhar
TIP: How to identify the samas type in ten seconds

Ask the questions in this order:
1. Does it start with prati / anu / yatha / bhar / aa and act as an adverb? Then Avyayibhav.
2. Does the first word denote a number? Then Dvigu.
3. Do both words matter equally, with an implied "and"? Then Dvandva.
4. Does the compound refer to a third person or thing not named in either word? Then Bahuvrihi.
5. Is one word an adjective of the other, both pointing to the same thing? Then Karmdharaya.
6. Otherwise, if the second word dominates, it is Tatpurush.

  • (c) Tatpurush
  • (d) Bahuvrihi <-- CORRECT
Why: "Dashanan" literally means "ten faces", but it actually denotes Ravana - a third entity named by neither component. That is the definition of Bahuvrihi. The trap is choosing Dvigu because "dash" is a number - but in Dvigu the compound denotes the *collection itself* (as in navratna, the nine gems), not a third thing.

  • (b) Dvandva <-- CORRECT
  • (c) Dvigu
  • (d) Bahuvrihi
Why: Both words are equally important and an "and" is implied between them, which is Dvandva. Such compounds are usually written with a hyphen - a useful visual clue.

TIP: The karak distinction that is always tested

Karan and Apadan both use the marker se, and telling them apart is the standard question.

Karan means "by means of" - an instrument. *Kalam se likha* (wrote with a pen).
Apadan means "from" - separation or origin. *Ped se patta gira* (the leaf fell from the tree).

Ask: is the se doing the work (Karan), or marking a source you are moving away from (Apadan)?

7.5.2 Ling and Vachan

  • Ling (gender) - Hindi has two: Pulling (masculine) and Stri-ling (feminine). Unlike English, inanimate objects also carry gender - *kitab* is feminine, *makan* is masculine.
  • Vachan (number) - Two: Ekvachan (singular) and Bahuvachan (plural). Common transformations: ladka to ladke, kitab to kitaben, nadi to nadiyan, chidiya to chidiyan.

7.8 Apathit Gadyansh - Unseen Passage

7.8.1 The method

The comprehension technique is identical to English - only the language changes.

*THE METHOD*

Step 1: Read the QUESTIONS first, so you know what to look for.
Step 2: Read the passage once for the overall subject and tone.
Step 3: Answer factual questions by locating the exact line.
Step 4: Answer the "shirshak" (title) question LAST, after you have
        understood the whole passage.
Step 5: For a title, choose the option that covers the WHOLE passage -
        not one paragraph, and not something broader than the passage.

GOLDEN RULE: the answer must come from the PASSAGE. If an option is
true in general knowledge but is not stated in the passage, it is wrong.
TIP: The title question in Hindi comprehension

"Upyukt shirshak" (most appropriate title) questions almost always appear, and candidates lose them by choosing the most *interesting* option rather than the most *representative* one.

Test each option against two questions: does it cover the entire passage, and does it claim more than the passage says? The right answer covers everything and claims nothing extra.
