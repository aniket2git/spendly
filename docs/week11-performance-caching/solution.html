Part 1 • Question 4What "performance" actually means

People say "the site is slow" as if slowness were one thing. It is not. There are three separate measurements, and mixing them up is the commonest mistake in this topic.

1.1 Latency and throughput are different things

Story box • the water pipe Two questions you can ask about a pipe:

"How long does one drop take to travel from one end to the other?" That is latency – a time, measured in milliseconds. Lower is better.

"How many litres come out per minute?" That is throughput – an amount per unit time, measured in requests per second. Higher is better.

A thin, very long pipe has bad latency. A short but narrow pipe has bad throughput. They are independent, and a good application needs both: many requests handled per second, each one answered quickly.

1.2 The third measurement: scalability

Story box • the one-window ticket counter A ticket counter serves one customer in 30 seconds. Lovely – when there is one customer. Now 200 people arrive at once. The clerk still takes 30 seconds each, so the last person waits 100 minutes.

Nothing about the clerk got slower. What failed was scalability: the ability to keep performing as the load grows. Measuring performance with one user tells you almost nothing about a real launch day.
Definition box • the three measurements
  • Latency: the time taken to serve one request. A duration. Lower is better.
  • Throughput: how many requests are served per unit time. A rate. Higher is better.
  • Scalability: whether latency and throughput hold up as the load grows.
All three are measurable numbers. That word matters for the question below.

1.3 What is NOT a performance criterion

Story box • judging a car by its badge You want to know how fast a car is. So you measure its top speed, its acceleration, and how it behaves fully loaded with luggage. What you do not do is say "it is a Maruti, therefore it is fast". The brand is not a measurement.

In the same way, "the app should be written in Flask or Django" is a statement about the tool chosen, not a measurement of speed. A brilliantly written Flask app and a terrible one are both Flask apps.

Part 3 • Questions 2 and 3Caching, and where caches live

Story box • the water bottle on your desk Every time you are thirsty you could walk to the kitchen tap. Instead you fill a bottle once and keep it on your desk. Now drinking takes one second instead of one minute.

That bottle is a cache: a nearby copy of something that lives far away, kept so you do not have to make the long trip again. The trade-off is the same one every cache has: the water in your bottle might be stale while the tap is always fresh. Speed is bought with the risk of staleness.
Definition box • cache A cache stores a copy of a resource closer to the consumer so future requests are served faster. A request answered from the cache is a cache hit; one that has to travel to the original source is a cache miss. The original source is called the origin.

3.1 The four places a cache can live

Why browser level has the least latency A browser cache hit involves no network at all – the file is read from your own disk. A proxy cache hit still requires a round trip out of your machine and onto the network, even if it is a short one. Distance is time, and zero distance wins.

3.3 What a hard refresh really does

Story box • emptying your own bottle You suspect the water in your desk bottle has gone stale, so you pour it away and refill it from the tap. Good. But your neighbour's bottle is still full of the old water, and the office water cooler in the corridor may also still hold yesterday's supply.

You control your own bottle only. A hard refresh empties your browser's cache for that page – and has no authority over the proxy in the middle.

Part 4 • Question 9ETag, If-Match and If-None-Match

Caching raises an obvious worry: how do I know my stored copy is still the current one? The answer is a small piece of HTTP called the ETag.

Story box • the version sticker on the notice The notice board holds a notice with a sticker on it reading v7. You photograph it and go home, remembering "I have v7".

Next week, instead of copying the whole notice again, you phone the office and ask: "is it still v7?"
  • "Yes, still v7" – a two-second call. You keep your photo. Nothing was re-copied.
  • "No, it is v8 now" – they read you the whole new notice.
That sticker is the ETag (short for entity tag). It is the server's version label for one particular version of one resource.
Definition box • ETag An ETag is a response header the server attaches to a resource, identifying that exact version of it. It is a string of ASCII characters written inside double quotes, for example ETag: "33a64df5". Usually it is a hash of the content, so any change to the file produces a different ETag.

4.1 The ETag round trip, drawn out

Why 304 matters so much A 304 Not Modified response carries no body. You still pay for one small round trip, but you avoid re-downloading the bytes. On a page with 40 images and stylesheets, that is the difference between reloading 2 MB and reloading almost nothing.

4.2 If-Match and If-None-Match are opposites

Both headers carry an ETag. The difference is the condition they set, and Question 9 turns on getting them the right way round.

The memory trick The header names say exactly what they mean, so trust them.
If-Match – "if it matches, proceed." Used when you are about to write.
If-None-Match – "if none match, send it." Used when you want to reuse a cache.

Part 5 • Question 1Flask-Caching: @cached vs @memoize

So far the caches have been outside our control. Now we cache inside our own Flask application, and there are two decorators that look interchangeable but are not.

5.1 The idea of a cache key

Story box • the labelled tiffin boxes Amma cooks and stores food in boxes on a shelf. To find food again she needs a label on each box. If every box is labelled just "FOOD", then the first box goes on the shelf and every later dish is assumed to be that same box – ask for rajma and you are handed the poha that got there first.

If instead the labels read "FOOD - rajma" and "FOOD - poha", everything works.

That label is the cache key. And the entire difference between the two decorators is what goes on the label.
Definition box • the two decorators
  • @cache.cached() builds its key from the request path (the default key is view/<request.path>). The function's parameters are not part of the key.
  • @cache.memoize() builds its key from the function plus its arguments. Different arguments therefore get different cache entries.

5.2 Seeing the bug happen, step by step

# THE BUG: @cached on a function that takes a parameter
@cache.cached(timeout=60)
def square(n):
    return n * n

square(2)   # cache miss -> computes 4, stores it under the path-based key
square(5)   # cache HIT on the SAME key -> returns 4    WRONG, should be 25

# THE FIX: @memoize puts the argument in the key
@cache.memoize(timeout=60)
def square(n):
    return n * n

square(2)   # miss -> 4  stored under  square + (2)
square(5)   # miss -> 25 stored under  square + (5)     CORRECT
square(2)   # hit  -> 4  from cache                    CORRECT and fast
Trap box • the direction students reverse It is very easy to remember "one of them includes the parameters" and then attach it to the wrong decorator. Anchor it with the word itself: memoize comes from memoization, which in computer science means caching a function's result per set of arguments. Arguments are baked into the very meaning of the word. So: memoize = arguments matter.

5.3 The special case: a function with no parameters

Story box • only one dish on the menu The whole problem with the label "FOOD" was that several different dishes competed for it. But suppose Amma only ever cooks one dish. Now the label "FOOD" is perfectly unambiguous – and a more detailed label would make no practical difference, because there is nothing to distinguish.

Same with our decorators. Their only difference is whether arguments join the key. Take away the arguments and the difference disappears.
Question 1 1 mark • select all that apply

Which of the following statements(s) is/are true regarding flask_caching?

  • A. The cache decorator includes the function parameters in the cache key.
  • B. The memoize decorator includes the function parameters in the cache key.
  • C. Both the cache and memoize decorators work the same way for functions not having any parameters.
  • D. All of the above

Option-by-option reasoning

OptionVerdictWhy
AFALSE The wrong way round. @cached builds its key from the request path (view/<request.path>), not from the function parameters. That is precisely why calling it with different arguments returns the first stored answer to everybody. Left panel of Figure 5.1.
BTRUE Correct, and it is the definition of memoization: the arguments are part of the cache key, so each distinct set of arguments gets its own entry.
CTRUE The only difference between them is whether arguments enter the key. For a function with no arguments there is nothing to include or exclude, so both produce a single cache entry and behave identically. Figure 5.2.
DFALSE Requires A to be true, and A is false. Note also that A and B are near-opposites: spotting that pair immediately tells you D cannot work.
Answer to Question 1 B and C
Matches your selection. A is false because @cached keys on the request path, not the parameters.
The practical rule for your own project
  • Caching a view function whose output depends only on the URL? Use @cached.
  • Caching any function whose result depends on its arguments? Use @memoize.
  • Both take a timeout in seconds. Clear entries with cache.delete_memoized(fn, args) when the underlying data changes.

Part 6 • Question 6Cookies: the hidden weight on every request

Story box • the identity card you must show every time Imagine a building where you must show your ID card at the door every single time you walk in – and you enter 80 times a day. If the card is a small plastic chip, fine. If it is a fat 4 KB folder of documents, you have just added that weight to all 80 entries.

A cookie is that ID card. The browser attaches it automatically to every request for that domain – every image, every stylesheet, every icon. So cookie size is multiplied by the number of requests, which is why it affects performance.
Definition box • cookie A cookie is a small piece of data the server asks the browser to store and send back on subsequent requests to that domain, in the Cookie request header. It is how a site remembers you are logged in. Cookies are sent automatically and on every matching request, whether that request needs them or not.

6.1 The domain attribute, and why subdomains matter

Story box • the family surname If a rule is written for "the Sharma family", it applies to every Sharma in the house – parents, children, cousins. If it is written for "Ravi Sharma only", it applies to him alone.

Setting a cookie's domain attribute to example.com is the family version: the cookie then travels to example.com and all its subdomains, including static.example.com, api.example.com and blog.example.com.
Question 6 1 mark • select all that apply

Which of the following is the correct statement?

  • A. Cookie size can impact the website performance.
  • B. Cookie size does not affect the website performance.
  • C. A request to static.example.com will include cookies set for example.com (assuming domain attribute is specified with "example.com").
  • D. A request to static.example.com will not include cookies set for example.com (assuming domain attribute is specified with "example.com").

Option-by-option reasoning

OptionVerdictWhy
ATRUE Cookies ride along on every request to the domain, so their size is multiplied by the number of requests. On a 50-request page a 4 KB cookie becomes 200 KB of pure overhead (Figure 6.1). Upload bandwidth is usually much slower than download, which makes this worse than it looks.
BFALSE Direct opposite of A, so exactly one of the pair is correct.
CTRUE Because the domain attribute was explicitly set to example.com, the cookie applies to that domain and its subdomains – so static.example.com receives it. Left panel of Figure 6.2.
DFALSE Opposite of C. It would be true only if the domain attribute had been omitted, which the question explicitly rules out.
Answer to Question 6 A and C
Matches your selection.
Trap box • the counter-intuitive bit Many students assume that naming a domain restricts the cookie. It does the opposite: specifying domain=example.com broadens it to every subdomain. If you want a cookie confined to one host, leave the attribute out altogether.

Part 7 • Question 7DOMContentLoaded vs load

Story box • the wedding hall You have to move the chairs in a wedding hall. Two moments you could wait for:

Moment 1: the hall is built, the floor is laid and all the furniture is in place. The chairs exist, so you can start moving them now.

Moment 2: everything above, plus the flowers have arrived, the lights are hung and the photographs are on the walls. Much later.

Moment 1 is DOMContentLoaded. Moment 2 is load. If your job is only to move a chair, waiting for the flowers is a waste of time.
Definition box • the two events
  • DOMContentLoaded fires as soon as the HTML has been parsed and the DOM tree is built. It does not wait for images or other subresources.
  • load fires only when the page and all its dependent resources – images, stylesheets, fonts, iframes – have finished loading.
DOMContentLoaded always fires first, often by a large margin.
Question 7 1 mark • single correct

Suppose you have a web page which includes a paragraph with some content, images and a stylesheet. You want to update the content of the paragraph with JavaScript. Which of the event, among load and DOMContentLoaded, you should wait to fire before updating the content for better performance?

  • A. load
  • B. DOMContentLoaded
  • C. Both of them will result in same performance.
  • D. DOM is not required to be loaded fully before updating the content.

Step-by-step reasoning

1What does the job actually need?
You want to change the text inside a <p> element. To do that, that element must exist in the DOM. That is the only requirement.
2Which event guarantees that, and nothing more?
DOMContentLoaded. It fires the moment the HTML is parsed and the DOM is built. The paragraph is present and editable from that instant.
3What would waiting for load add?
It would additionally wait for every image and stylesheet to finish downloading. Your paragraph text has nothing to do with the photographs, so that waiting is pure delay – 2.2 seconds of it in Figure 7.1.
4Conclusion.
Wait for the earliest event that satisfies your requirement. That is DOMContentLoaded, so the user sees the updated text sooner.

Option-by-option reasoning

OptionVerdictWhy
AFALSE load works, but it is slower. The question asks for better performance, so "it works" is not enough.
BTRUE
the answer		 Fires as soon as the DOM is ready, which is all the task needs. Earliest safe moment means best performance.
CFALSE They differ, often by seconds, because load waits for images. Figure 7.1 shows the gap.
DFALSE You cannot edit an element that does not exist yet. Running the script before the DOM is built gives you null and a JavaScript error – the classic "Cannot set property of null" mistake.
Answer to Question 7 Option B – DOMContentLoaded
Matches your selection. 
// the right way: act as soon as the DOM exists

Part 8 • Question 8Images: the biggest easy win

Story box • the giant poster and the small frame You need a photograph to fit a small 10 cm frame. Two ways:

The wasteful way: order a huge 20 cm poster, carry it home, then fold it down to fit the frame. You paid for paper you threw away, you carried extra weight, and you did extra work folding it.

The sensible way: order a 10 cm print. Less to carry, nothing wasted, no folding.

Setting width="100" on a 200 px image is the folding. The browser still downloads every byte of the big image, then does extra work shrinking it for display.
The arithmetic worth remembering A 200 x 200 image holds 40 000 pixels. A 100 x 100 image holds 10 000. That is four times as much pixel data, not twice – because both dimensions doubled. This is why oversized images are usually the single heaviest thing on a slow web page.

Part 9 • Question 10CDN: a copy near every user

Story box • one factory, or shops in every town A biscuit company has one factory in Chennai. If every customer in India had to travel to Chennai for a packet of biscuits, buying biscuits would be slow and the factory gate would be permanently jammed.

So the company opens shops in every town. Same biscuits, made in the same factory, but now a customer in Jaipur walks 5 minutes instead of travelling 2000 km.

That network of shops is a CDN. The factory is your origin server; each shop is an edge server. Nothing about the content changes – only the distance does.
Definition box • CDN CDN stands for Content Delivery Network. It is a geographically distributed network of servers that keeps cached copies of your content close to users, so content is delivered faster. The nearby servers are called edge servers or points of presence.

10.3 The one-page cheat sheet

Measuring performance

  • Latency: time for one request. A duration. Lower is better.
  • Throughput: requests served per unit time. A rate. Higher is better.
  • Scalability: whether both hold up as load grows.
  • A framework choice is not a performance criterion. If you cannot put a number on it, it does not count.
  • Tools: Lighthouse, GTmetrix, WebPageTest, PageSpeed Insights, DevTools. Postman is for API testing, not page performance.

Caching

  • Four levels: browser (private), proxy (shared), CDN edge (shared, geographic), server-side (Flask-Caching + Redis).
  • Browser level has the least latency – no network journey at all.
  • A proxy cache serves its copy to multiple users. That is what "shared" means.
  • Hard refresh clears your browser's cache for that page. It has no power over a proxy, which may still answer from its own copy.
  • Cache hit = served from cache. Cache miss = had to go to the origin.

ETag and conditional requests

  • ETag: a response header identifying a version of a resource; an ASCII string in double quotes, e.g. "33a64df5".
  • If-None-Match: "send it only if it has changed". Match means 304 Not Modified with no body. This is the caching workhorse.
  • If-Match: "proceed only if it matches". No match means 412 Precondition Failed. Used for safe writes.
  • Date-based cousins: Last-Modified and If-Modified-Since.

Flask-Caching

  • @cached: key is the request path (view/<path>). Parameters are not in the key.
  • @memoize: key includes the function arguments. Use it for any function whose result depends on its inputs.
  • For a function with no parameters, the two behave identically.
  • Using @cached on a parameterised function is a real bug: every argument collides on one entry.

Front-end details

  • Cookies are attached to every request for the domain, so size matters. Fix: a cookie-free domain for static assets.
  • domain=example.com makes the cookie reach example.com and all subdomains, including static.example.com. Omitting the attribute keeps it to that one host.
  • DOMContentLoaded: DOM is built. Fires first. load: everything including images is done. Fires later.
  • Only need the DOM? Use DOMContentLoaded.
  • Never scale images down in HTML. 200x200 is four times the pixels of 100x100, plus resizing work.

CDN

  • Content Delivery Network: a distributed network of servers holding cached copies.
  • It works by reducing the distance between client and resource.
  • Side benefit: the origin server's load drops sharply.
  • The nearby servers are edge servers or points of presence.

10.5 Practice questions

Cover the answers. If you can do these eight, you have genuinely learned Week 11.

P1. You write @cache.cached(timeout=60) above def get_marks(student_id). Ravi (id 1) loads his marks, then Sita (id 2) loads hers. What does Sita see, and why? What is the fix?

P2. A colleague says "I hard refreshed and I still see the old logo, so caching is broken." Give two possible explanations.

P3. Your page makes 60 requests to myshop.com and your session cookie is 2 KB. How many bytes of cookie overhead per page load? How would you reduce it?

P4. A server responds 304 Not Modified. What did the browser send to cause this, and roughly how many bytes of body came back?

P5. Which is faster to serve and why: a resource cached in your browser, or the same resource cached at your ISP's proxy?

P6. You need JavaScript to read the final rendered height in pixels of a photo on the page. Should you use DOMContentLoaded or load? Why is this different from Question 7?

P7. You set Set-Cookie: sid=xyz; domain=example.com. List which of these receive the cookie: example.com, static.example.com, example.org, a.b.example.com.

P8. An image is displayed at 300x300. You have a 900x900 file. Roughly how many times more pixel data are you sending than necessary?

Answers to the practice questions

P1. Sita sees Ravi's marks. @cached keys on the request path, not on student_id, so both calls collide on one cache entry and the first stored result is returned to everyone. The fix is @cache.memoize(timeout=60), which puts student_id into the key. (This is also a security bug, not merely a performance one.)

P2. (1) A proxy cache between them and the server still holds the old file – their refresh only cleared their own browser. (2) The logo is referenced by an unchanged URL and is being served from a CDN edge that has not yet expired its copy. A third possibility: they refreshed a different page from the one that embeds the logo. The standard cure is cache busting – rename the file to logo.v2.png or add ?v=2.

P3. 60 x 2 KB = 120 KB of cookie sent per page load, all of it upload traffic. Reduce it by serving static assets from a cookie-free domain (or a CDN hostname), and by storing only a small session id in the cookie rather than user data.

P4. The browser sent a conditional request with If-None-Match: "<etag>" (or If-Modified-Since). The 304 carries no body at all – only headers, a few hundred bytes. The browser then reuses its stored copy.

P5. The browser copy. It is read from local disk with no network round trip whatsoever, whereas the proxy still needs a request to leave your machine and come back. Zero distance beats short distance.

P6. load. Reading a photo's rendered height requires the image to have actually downloaded, because until then the browser does not know its dimensions. Question 7 only edited text, which needs nothing but the DOM. The rule is: wait for the earliest event that genuinely satisfies your dependency.

P7. Receive it: example.com, static.example.com, and a.b.example.com (all subdomains at any depth). Does not: example.org – a completely different domain.

P8. 900x900 = 810 000 pixels; 300x300 = 90 000 pixels. That is 9 times the pixel data (because 3 x 3), all downloaded and then thrown away during resizing.

10.7 Final word

If you remember only seven sentences from this whole book
  1. Performance is numbers: latency, throughput, scalability. A framework name is not a number.
  2. Lighthouse and GTmetrix measure pages; Postman tests APIs.
  3. Caches live at browser, proxy, CDN and server level, and browser level has the least latency.
  4. A hard refresh clears only your own cache – a proxy can still serve you a stored copy.
  5. ETag + If-None-Match gets you a body-free 304; If-Match means "only if it matches" and fails with 412.
  6. memoize keys on arguments, cached keys on the path, and with no arguments they are the same.
  7. Small cookies, correctly sized images, DOMContentLoaded over load, and a CDN to shorten the distance.
